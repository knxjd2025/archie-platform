Module 1

Safety on the roof

Flat roofs feel safe. They are not. Read this before you go up on any commercial job.

Rule one: no inspection, photo, or sale is worth a fall. If something feels wrong on a roof, stop and get down.
  • Never go up alone. Text the office the address and when you go up and come down.
  • Check in first. Meet the building manager. Ask about roof access, alarms on the hatch, and anything dangerous up top.
  • Ladders: tie it off, keep 3 points of contact, and the ladder should stick up 3 feet past the roof edge.
  • Stay back from edges. Keep at least 15 feet from any edge with no parapet or rail. Closer than that, you need fall protection. Follow your company's fall protection rule every time.
  • Skylights are holes. Never step on, sit on, or lean on a skylight or plastic panel. People die falling through them.
  • Slick surfaces. Dew, frost, or rain on TPO or PVC is like ice. Wait for it to dry.
  • Watch your feet. Soft spots, ridges, wires, gas lines, and conduit are trip and fall points.
  • Stay off equipment. Don't open HVAC panels or touch electrical. Hot exhaust and grease vents can burn you.
  • Heat. Membrane can get well over 150°F in summer. Bring water and go up early.

Know the roof types

Every commercial roof falls into a few families. Learn to spot each one in under a minute.

The first thing you do on any commercial roof is figure out what it is. Everything else (what to look for, what it costs, what we can offer) depends on this.

Module 3

How to walk a commercial roof

Same order every time, so nothing gets missed.

  1. Before you go

    Ask the owner or manager: How old is the roof? Any repairs? Where are the leaks? Any warranty? Pack your seam probe, tape measure, chalk or marker, phone with a full battery, and the drone if you have it.

  2. Start inside

    Walk the inside first. Look for ceiling stains, wet tiles, buckets, rust on the metal deck, and mold. Mark each leak on a quick sketch so you can find that spot from above.

  3. Overview photos

    Once you are up, take wide photos from each corner. Get drone shots if you can. These show the whole roof before you zoom in.

  4. Walk the edges

    Go all the way around the perimeter. Check edge metal, coping on top of walls, parapet flashing, gutters, and scuppers. Wind damage starts here.

  5. Walk the field in rows

    Walk back and forth like mowing a lawn. Look for dirt rings (old ponding), blisters, soft spots, punctures, wrinkles, and patches.

  6. Check the seams

    On TPO and PVC, run the probe along seams, especially at T-joints and corners. On EPDM and mod bit, look for laps lifting or sealant missing.

  7. Check every penetration

    Pipes, vents, pitch pans, HVAC curbs, skylights, and the roof hatch. Most leaks come from these spots, not the open field.

  8. Check the drains

    Is the drain clear? Is the strainer (dome) on? Is the clamping ring tight? Look for water lines and debris around it.

  9. Check rooftop equipment

    Look for missing HVAC panels, leaking condensate lines, grease from kitchen fans, and worn paths where techs walk.

  10. Measure and core

    Measure the roof. With the owner's OK, cut a core to see how many roofs are on it, how thick the insulation is, the deck type, and if it is wet. Patch it right away.

  11. Write it up the same day

    Put photos in order: wide shot, then close up, for every problem. Notes get fuzzy fast. Do it today.

Module 4

What good looks like

These are the details a correct roof should have. If it doesn't match, it goes in your report.

1.5 in

TPO weld width

Welded seams need at least 1.5 inches of true weld (Versico spec). Less than that should be overlaid, even if it probes OK.

Probe

Seam probe test

Drag a dull probe along the seam edge. On a good weld it will not go in. If it slides into the lap, that is a void. Mark the start and end and re-weld. Wait about 30 minutes after welding before probing new seams.

8 in

Base flashing height

Flashing at walls and curbs should go at least 8 inches above the finished roof (NRCA guideline). Short flashing lets water run over the top.

48 hr

Ponding rule

Water still sitting 48 hours after rain is ponding. Look for dirt rings and stains on a dry day.

ES-1

Edge metal

Edge metal should be tight, fastened, with joints covered and membrane stripped in. The building code calls for edge systems tested to ANSI/SPRI ES-1 on most low-slope roofs.

Boots

Pipes and penetrations

Best case is a pre-made boot or flashing with a clamp. Pitch pans should be full and crowned so water sheds off the top.

Term bar

Wall terminations

Membrane at the top of the flashing is held with a fastened termination bar, sealant on top, and counterflashing or a cap over it.

Low point

Drains

Drains sit at the low spots. Strainer on, clamping ring tight, membrane sealed under the ring. Tapered insulation should move water to them.

Module 5

Problem library

The issues you will find most. What they look like, why they happen, and what to do.

"All roofs" issues show under every filter.

Open seams and fishmouths

High risk
What you see
Seam edges lifting, gaps your probe slides into, little open tunnels (fishmouths) at the edge.
Why it happens
Cold welds, dirty seams, glue failing with age, the sheet moving.
What to do
Probe and mark it. Photo close and wide. On TPO/PVC re-weld or strip over. On EPDM clean, prime, and tape.

Ponding water

High risk
What you see
Standing water, or on a dry day: dirt rings, algae, stains, sagging areas.
Why it happens
Low spots, clogged drains, deck sagging, crushed or wet insulation, poor slope.
What to do
Note size and depth. Check drains first. Fix may be crickets, tapered insulation, or a new drain. Most warranties don't cover ponding damage.

Blisters

Medium risk
What you see
Bubbles or soft raised spots, mostly on mod bit and BUR. Can be fist size or bigger.
Why it happens
Air or moisture trapped between layers. Heat makes it grow.
What to do
Don't step on them. Small and intact can be watched. Broken or in a lap gets cut out and patched. Lots of them means moisture, so check for wet insulation.

Cracks, splits, and alligatoring

High risk
What you see
Cracked surface like alligator skin, splits across the sheet, crazing on old single-ply.
Why it happens
Sun and age dry the material out. Movement in the deck or insulation.
What to do
Surface crazing can be coated if the roof is dry. Splits through the sheet leak now and need patching. Wide-spread cracking means end of life.

EPDM shrinkage and bridging

High risk
What you see
Sheet pulling away from walls and curbs, flashing stretched tight in a gap at the corner (bridging), seams being pulled apart.
Why it happens
EPDM loses oils and shrinks as it ages, more on ballasted or loose roofs.
What to do
Photo the gap. Small areas can be re-flashed. Bad shrinkage across the roof usually means replacement.

Punctures and foot traffic damage

High risk
What you see
Holes, cuts, dents, screws or debris in the sheet, worn paths near HVAC units.
Why it happens
Dropped tools, HVAC techs, other trades, hail, debris.
What to do
Mark every one with chalk and photo it. Patch. Suggest walk pads on paths to equipment.

Flashing pulled away

High risk
What you see
Flashing peeling off walls or curbs, missing term bar, open corners, sealant cracked or gone.
Why it happens
Bad install, shrinkage, wall movement, old sealant.
What to do
Most leaks live here. Photo the top edge and corners. Re-secure, re-flash, add counterflashing.

Failed pitch pans

Medium risk
What you see
Pitch pan filler shrunk, cracked, or pulled from the pipe. Water can sit in the pan.
Why it happens
Filler dries and shrinks over time. Often no one maintains them.
What to do
Top off and crown the filler. Better: swap for a pre-made boot or sealed curb when possible.

Clogged drains and scuppers

High risk
What you see
Leaves and trash on the strainer, missing strainer, water lines around drains, rusted or split scupper boxes.
Why it happens
No maintenance, trees nearby, bad scupper flashing.
What to do
Clear it while you are there if safe. A blocked drain can let water build up and add a lot of weight on the roof. Recommend a maintenance plan.

Wet insulation

High risk
What you see
Soft or spongy areas when you walk, membrane that crunches or squishes, stains inside with no clear hole above.
Why it happens
Old leaks that have been soaking in for months or years.
What to do
Mark soft areas. Core with permission. Recommend an infrared or moisture survey. Wet insulation must be cut out; it can't be coated over.

Edge metal and coping failure

High risk
What you see
Loose or lifted edge metal, open joints, missing cleats, coping caps sliding or missing, rust.
Why it happens
Wind, poor fastening, old sealant, metal moving with heat.
What to do
Treat this as urgent. Wind gets under a loose edge and can peel back the roof. Re-fasten, strip in, or replace edge metal.

Wind uplift and loose membrane

High risk
What you see
Membrane puffing or billowing, popped fasteners pushing up, lifted corners, moved ballast rock.
Why it happens
Weak edge or fastening, high wind events.
What to do
Document for a possible claim. Check fastener rows and edges. Loose areas need re-securing before the next storm.

Hail damage

Medium risk
What you see
Dents in vents, caps, HVAC fins, and edge metal. Cracks or star breaks in the membrane. Soft or crushed spots in insulation.
Why it happens
Storm. Older and colder membranes break easier.
What to do
Follow the storm steps in Module 6. Measure dents on soft metals to show hail size. Core to check crushed insulation.

Metal roof fasteners and rust

Medium risk
What you see
Screws backed out, cracked or missing washers, rust around screws and laps, holes.
Why it happens
Metal moves with heat every day, which works screws loose. Washers break down in the sun.
What to do
Photo screws close up. Fix may be new oversized screws, sealing laps, or a coating system.

HVAC and rooftop equipment

Medium risk
What you see
Panels left on the roof, condensate dripping on the membrane, grease around kitchen fans, units sitting on low curbs.
Why it happens
Other trades, no walk pads, grease breaks down many membranes.
What to do
Note equipment type and data plate. Grease areas may need a grease-resistant sheet like PVC. Raise low curbs during replacement.

Wrinkles and ridges

Low risk
What you see
Long humps or wrinkles in the membrane, ridges over insulation board joints.
Why it happens
Board movement, sheet not relaxed before install, wet insulation.
What to do
Usually not urgent by itself. A wrinkle crossing a seam can open it up later. Ridges plus soft spots means check for moisture.

Material mix-ups

Medium risk
What you see
PVC touching asphalt or old tar, wrong sealant on a sheet, sticky or discolored membrane at contact points.
Why it happens
Repairs done with the wrong products.
What to do
Note it in the report. Repairs must use products made for that membrane.

Module 6

Storm damage and insurance

How to find and document hail and wind damage so the claim holds up.

Hail

  • Start with soft metals. Vent caps, HVAC fins, pipe caps, edge metal. Dents prove hail hit and show the size. Measure and photo with a tape in the shot.
  • Then the membrane. Look for cracks, star breaks, and bruises. Old or cold single-ply cracks easier. Mark each hit with chalk.
  • Then under the membrane. Press on hit spots. Soft or crushed feeling means the insulation or cover board may be fractured. Core with the owner's OK to show it.
  • Check the date. Pull the storm date and hail size for that address so photos match the event.

Wind

  • Edges and corners first. Lifted edge metal, missing coping, and peeled corners are the most common wind damage.
  • Field of the roof. Loose or billowing membrane, fasteners pushing up through the sheet, moved ballast.
  • Debris. Branches, other building parts, or equipment that blew across the roof and caused punctures.
  • Interior. New leaks right after the storm help tie damage to the date.
Photo order for every claim item: 1) wide shot that shows where it is on the roof, 2) medium shot, 3) close up with a tape measure or chalk circle for scale. Same order every time so the adjuster can follow it.

Module 7

Repair, restore, or replace

Use what you found to give the owner the right option.

Repair

Fix the problem spots

  • Problems are in a few spots, like a seam, a curb, or a drain.
  • The rest of the membrane is in good shape.
  • Insulation is dry.
  • Roof has good life left.
Restore

Coat or recover over it

  • Membrane is aging but still holding.
  • Insulation is mostly dry. Confirm with a moisture survey and cores; wet spots get cut out first.
  • Owner wants to push replacement off for years at a lower cost.
  • A recover is only allowed if there is just one roof on it now.
Replace

Tear off and start new

  • Wet insulation in large areas.
  • Membrane is at end of life: cracking, shrinking, seams failing all over.
  • Already two roofs on it. The building code does not allow a third; it must be torn off.
  • Deck damage or rust found in cores.
